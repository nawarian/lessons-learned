Forward & Backward

The two passes that are learning — and why forward() and backward() are two halves of one machine.

The win: by the end of this lesson you will be able to point at any line in your training loop and say exactly which of the two passes it belongs to, and what it does to the model's weights. The mystery of forward() and loss.backward() disappears.

Should you learn this now or later?

Now. You guessed right on two counts: these are PyTorch mechanics, and they are "forward propagation" and "backward propagation." But this isn't a framework detail to defer — it is the literal answer to your mission's central question, "how does training really work?" Everything else in the pipeline (the tokenizer, attention, the optimizer) is plumbing around this one idea. The original plan put it at Lesson 6, but you ran the code and asked the right question at the right moment, so we're pulling it forward.

One machine, two passes

A neural network learns by repeating a two-step cycle, millions of times. Think of a student taking practice exams:

→ Forward pass

Run the input through the model to get a prediction. "Here are my answers."

Also called forward propagation. This is what your forward() method defines.

← Backward pass

Work backward from the score to figure out which habits caused each mistake, and how to adjust them. "Here's how I should study differently."

Also called backpropagation. This is what loss.backward() runs.

The two are inseparable: you cannot grade an exam you never took. The backward pass can only run because the forward pass recorded what it did on the way through. That recording is the link you were sensing between the two methods.

The forward pass: nn.Module and forward()

nn.Module is PyTorch's base class for anything that holds learnable weights. When your GPT class subclasses it, you get three things for free:[1]

  • Parameter tracking — every nn.Linear, nn.Embedding, etc. you assign as an attribute is auto-registered, so model.parameters() (the thing you hand to the optimizer) finds all of them.
  • Device & mode plumbing — .to(DEVICE), .train(), .eval() all walk that registry for you.
  • The __call__ hook — this is the one that explains a line that probably looked odd.

You define forward(), but you never call it directly. In the training loop you wrote logits = model(x), not model.forward(x). That's deliberate:

Calling the module — model(x) — runs nn.Module.__call__, which fires registered hooks and sets up bookkeeping, then calls your forward(). Calling forward() directly skips that wrapper. Rule: define forward, but always invoke the module.

Your forward() is just the recipe for turning input IDs into logits — embed, add positions, run the blocks, project to vocabulary: 

def forward(self, x):              # x: (B, T) token IDs
    B, T = x.shape
    tok_emb = self.token_embedding(x)
    pos_emb = self.pos_embedding(torch.arange(T, device=x.device))
    x = tok_emb + pos_emb
    for block in self.blocks:
        x = block(x)
    x = self.ln_f(x)
    return self.head(x)            # (B, T, VOCAB_SIZE) logits

Here is the part that makes the backward pass possible. As each of those operations runs, PyTorch's autograd engine quietly records it into a computation graph — a map of "this tensor was produced from those tensors, by this operation."[2] The forward pass doesn't just compute the answer; it leaves a breadcrumb trail back to every weight that influenced it.

What's actually in logits — and why every position predicts

A trap worth defusing before we go on: logits are not the weights, and they are not probabilities. The weights are the learned machinery inside the layers; the logits are what comes out when input flows through that machinery — raw scores, one per vocabulary entry. They can be negative, don't sum to 1, and only become probabilities after softmax (which is exactly the next step in your generate()): 

logits = model(x)               # raw scores, e.g. [2.1, -0.4, 5.8, ...]  shape (B, T, 65)
probs  = F.softmax(logits, -1)  # now a distribution: [0.04, 0.01, 0.71, ...]  sums to 1

The shape (B, T, VOCAB_SIZE) is about your data, not your layers: B sequences × T positions × 65 scores. The number of transformer layers never appears — they're passed through. And note what the T axis means: the model emits a next-character guess at every position at once, not just the last one.

The targets are the input, shifted left by one

Take "The quick brown " (16 characters). One forward pass produces 16 independent next-char guesses; the true answer for each position is simply the next character — which is your CharDataset's y = data[i+1 : i+T+1]:

input x: T h e ' ' q u i c k ' ' b r o w n ' ' target y: h e ' ' q u i c k ' ' b r o w n ' ' (f) └─ "after T predict h" "after the whole prefix predict f" ─┘

Predicting the f of fox is just the last column. The other 15 columns re-predict characters you already have — useful because in training we know every answer and use them all as teaching signal. (And remember: char-level, so it guesses the next character, not the next word.)

One pass, not 16 — the causal mask does it

The model does not chop the input into 16 growing prefixes and run them separately. The whole sequence goes through once, and a causal mask — the torch.tril line in your SelfAttention — blinds each position to everything on its right, so each position behaves as if it only saw the characters up to itself:

can attend to → T h e ' ' q pos 0 T [ ✓ · · · · ] sees: T pos 1 h [ ✓ ✓ · · · ] sees: T h pos 2 e [ ✓ ✓ ✓ · · ] sees: T h e pos 3 ' ' [ ✓ ✓ ✓ ✓ · ] sees: T h e ' ' pos 4 q [ ✓ ✓ ✓ ✓ ✓ ] sees: T h e ' ' q

So you get exactly the behaviour you'd expect from 16 growing prefixes, but from a single matrix multiply — no Python loop, no chunking. This mask is the heart of Lesson 4 (Attention); for now just know it's what makes the parallel prediction possible.

Why bother predicting at every position?

Reason 1 — efficiency. A 16-character sequence secretly contains 16 complete "guess the next character" problems. Predict only at the last position and one forward pass earns you one gradient signal; predict at all positions and the same matrix multiply earns you 16. That's why the loss flattens to (B*T, 65) and averages — maximum learning per unit of compute.

Reason 2 — the mask keeps it honest. Position 2 must predict character 3, which is sitting right there in the input. Without the mask it would learn the useless rule "copy the next input" — perfect accuracy, zero understanding. Blinding each position to its own answer forces real prediction. So the mask both enables the parallel training and keeps every one of those 16 signals valid.

This only works in training, where we already know the answers (called teacher forcing). Generation can't — char 17 doesn't exist until char 16 is chosen — so it runs strictly one position at a time.

The loss: collapsing everything to one number

The forward pass gives you logits — a score for every possible next token, at every position. F.cross_entropy compares those against the true next tokens and collapses the whole batch into a single scalar: how surprised the model was. High loss = bad predictions.

That single number is the root of the computation graph. It is the thing you want to make smaller, and the starting point for the walk backward.

The backward pass: loss.backward()

Calling .backward() on the loss tells autograd to walk the recorded graph in reverse, from the loss back to every weight, applying the chain rule from calculus at each step.[3] The result it computes for each weight is a gradient:

"If I nudge this one weight up by a hair, does the loss go up or down — and how steeply?"

After loss.backward() returns, every parameter p has its answer sitting in p.grad. No weights have changed yet — you've only computed the directions. Picture the loss as a landscape and each gradient as an arrow pointing uphill; to reduce loss you'll step the opposite way. That stepping is the optimizer's job, not backward's.

The three-line dance, mapped to your code

Every training step in your script is the same three moves, in this exact order:

optimizer.zero_grad()Wipe last step's gradients. PyTorch accumulates into .grad by default, so you must clear first.
loss.backward()Backward pass. Fill every p.grad by walking the graph the forward pass recorded.
optimizer.step()Nudge every weight a little, opposite its gradient. AdamW decides the exact step size.

Forgetting zero_grad() is the classic beginner bug: gradients from old batches pile on top of new ones and training quietly destabilizes. The order is not negotiable — you can't step before you know the direction, and you can't find the direction without the forward pass that came just before these three lines.
